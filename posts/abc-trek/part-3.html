Pursuit of the Foothills of Annapurna · Part III

Storm at Chhomrong, Sunrise on the Fishtail

Chhomrong · Sinuwa · Bamboo · Dovan

April 2026 7 min read

Chhomrong welcomed us with rain at its harshest, accompanied by sharp bursts of hail. We rushed for cover and found shelter in a nearby lodge, watching the storm unfold in full force around us. The mountains were testing our resolve from the moment we arrived, quietly reminding us that nothing here is given freely and every bit of beauty and clarity had to be earned.

As the rain began to ease, we stepped out again, as we had one task left, we had to get ourselves checked in at the entry post. With bodies drained and movements slow, every step felt heavier than the last and all we could think of was finding a place to rest.

We got the formalities done, and without much thought, booked a room at the nearest lodge we could find.

By then, the storm had passed.

A cool mountain breeze began to flow throughout the lands, and the clouds slowly started to drift away, revealing what they had been hiding all along. Across the valley, the white peaks emerged, quiet and untouched, standing in complete contrast to the chaos that had just passed.

When we entered the room, we realized it opened directly towards Machapuchare. Even in that moment, tired as we were, a thought lingered in the back of our minds, the sunrise from here would be something special.

We dropped our bags almost instantly and lay down for a while, letting the body finally catch up. By the time lunch was being prepared, we had freshened up a bit.

At lunch, I decided to try the well-known garlic soup, something almost every trekker swears by in these altitudes. Warm, simple, and unexpectedly comforting, it felt less like food and more like medicine. Over the next few days, it would quietly become one of the most reliable companions on the journey.

As the sun began its slow descent, the mountains seemed to bid us farewell for the day, their silhouettes softening as dusk quietly took over. The sky dimmed in gentle hues, and with it, the long day finally came to rest.

We gathered in the dining room, settling into warmth and conversation. Stories flowed easily between trekkers, strangers just hours ago, now bound by the shared rhythm of the trail. Laughter echoed through every corner, filling the space with a comforting sense of togetherness. Outside, the cold tightened its grip, the temperature slipping into single digits, but inside, there was only warmth. As night deepened, one by one, everyone retreated to their rooms, carrying with them the quiet satisfaction of a sleep much needed and well earned.

Morning arrived softly. I woke from one of the most restful sleeps in recent memory, deep, uninterrupted. As I looked out of the window the first thing I could see was Machapuchare, who rose in absolute prominence, as its sharp, elegant peak was catching the first light of day as if it owned the sky itself. Just beside it, Annapurna South and Hiunchuli stood in quiet grandeur, completing a panorama that felt almost unreal.

Cameras came out instantly, tripods followed, and for a while, nothing else mattered but capturing the grace of those peaks. Once the traditional morning rituals were complete, we prepared ourselves for the day ahead.

From this side of the valley, the trail revealed itself clearly. Sinuwa stood across the gorge, waiting. Between us and it lay the familiar challenge, steep descents, relentless stairs, and the promise of another long climb. We stood there for a moment, looking across, tracing the path with our eyes, imagining what lay ahead.

And then, with fresh minds and renewed energy, we turned away, bidding Chhomrong a temporary goodbye, and stepped forward once again.

The trail from Chhomrong began with a long, steady descent of about 200meters. For the first 30-35 minutes, we kept moving downward until we reached the Chhomrong Gumba, a quiet Buddhist temple resting peacefully against a majestic mountain backdrop. It felt like a brief pause, a moment of calm before the trail unfolded further.

Continuing the descent for another stretch of 30 mins, we eventually arrived at the second and final suspension bridge of the trek. As we crossed, the familiar mountain breeze returned, cool and refreshing, weaving through the valley.

On the other side, the climb began.

The ascent towards Sinuwa rose steep and unrelenting, another test of patience and endurance. Somewhere along one of the countless staircases, my attention drifted to a cat wandering nearby. That was enough to break my rhythm. I stopped, gave in to the moment, and spent a few minutes playing with it until it had clearly had enough and walked away, unimpressed.

The trail, however, had more companions to offer. We stopped at a spot near Upper Sinuwa, where a giant husky lay resting, calm and unbothered. There was no walking past this one. We paused, gave in to the moment, and spent a while playing with it, much to its mild annoyance. Along the way, we met many such furry guides, mostly dogs, who seemed to appear out of nowhere and walk alongside us for a while. They asked for nothing more than a few pets, perhaps a biscuit if we had one, and in return, offered quiet company on an otherwise demanding climb.

We reached Upper Sinuwa at around 11:30 in the morning, a journey that took us roughly three and a half hours. The moment we arrived, it felt like stepping into an entirely different world.

Unlike Chhomrong, which greets you with towering mountain views, Sinuwa opens itself up towards the horizon. Layers of lush green hills stretched endlessly across the valley, each ridge gently fading into the next, with the Kali Gandaki River carving its quiet path far below.

Standing at an elevation of 2360 meters, we could clearly see Chhomrong resting on the opposite side of the valley. From this vantage point, it gave us a quiet sense of accomplishment in that moment, a realization of how far we had come. But much of the journey still remained.

We rested at Sinuwa for a while, letting our legs recover, and then set off again. This stretch of the trek led us deep into dense bamboo and rhododendron forests, where the light filtered softly through the foliage and the trail felt more enclosed.

The route from Sinuwa to Bamboo was among the gentler sections of the journey. The path proceeded in a steady rhythm with the slopes of the hills, ascending and descending in gradual waves, never too harsh, yet never entirely easy.

Each time we found ourselves descending for more than a few minutes, it almost certainly meant a stream awaited below, cascading down the slopes in clear, cold flows.

And with every descent came the quiet certainty of the climb that would follow. Each crossing carried the promise of another ascent, as the trail moved in a steady rhythm through the forest. For a long while, all we could see were trees, dense and enclosing, until a small clearing opened up, revealing what looked like an unused well.

I stopped to reorganize a few things. The moment I did, a cold mountain wind rushed in from the valley above, sudden and forceful, throwing everything into disarray. I hurriedly gathered my belongings and stuffed them back into my backpack, working against the relentless gusts.

Once done, I took a few steps forward and paused. With my arms stretched wide, I let the wind rush past me, embracing it fully. And as it slowly began to settle, my eyes latched on to the blue rooftops far in the distance. Anyone trekking in these parts of the world would know that the sight of any building is quite a reassuring one. Upon asking a passerby, I learnt that the group of rooftops far ahead was actually Dovan.

Rested and quietly reinvigorated, I resumed walking. My companions were already far ahead. As I moved along, I found myself walking with other trekkers, sharing brief stories, exchanging greetings, all of us weaving through the forest in the same quiet pursuit.

Eventually, I reached Bamboo, where my companions were waiting at a nearby lodge, holding off on ordering lunch until I arrived. The meal was simple. A couple of fried eggs, just enough to refuel, before we set off again toward Dovan.

The trail ahead felt familiar. Forested stretches, at times accompanied by the same old stone stairs, and the same steady progression forward. We had planned to push further and reach Himalaya by the end of the day, but the mountains had other plans.

Rain began to fall.

With the weather turning, we decided to stop at Dovan instead. And once again, under a steady curtain of rain, we made our way forward and arrived at Dovan….
