Pursuit of the Foothills of Annapurna · Part II

The Mountains Ask for Their Due

Pokhara · Nayapul · Jhinu Danda · Chhomrong

April 2026 5 min read

The journey from Kathmandu to Pokhara is a long one, stretching roughly 200 kilometres and taking at least 8 to 9 hours. Travelling on Nepal’s famous sofa buses makes the distance a bit easier to endure, and while a journey of that length may sound monotonous, the road itself keeps you company and rarely lets boredom settle in, unless you are travelling at night, which we usually prefer to save time.

And while an 8–9 hour journey might sound monotonous on paper, the road has a way of keeping you engaged. It twists and turns through hills while the rivers keep you company from time to time, constantly changing, never quite letting you settle into boredom. Unless, of course, you take the night bus, which we usually do, more out of practicality than preference, trying to save time wherever we can.

Following winding mountain roads, occasionally passing through stretches of construction, and eventually coming to a complete halt for nearly four hours due to highway maintenance, the journey felt longer than it should have.

As the first light of the day appeared, and the clock struck 6, we finally reached Pokhara, as we inched closer to the start of our real journey. We reached the Tourist Bus Park and sat down for breakfast at a small shop run by a charismatic old uncle, who seems to welcome every traveller like a friend, calling them in with an energy that’s hard to ignore.

Anytime we pass through the Tourist Bus Park, it has almost become a ritual to stop by his place, share a few laughs, exchange a few words with the old man, and ease into the journey ahead. This time was no different.

After breakfast, a cup of tea, and a bit of freshening up, we were ready to move again. We took a taxi to the jeep stand, and from there, began the next leg of our journey, heading towards Jhinu Danda.

The road into the Annapurna Conservation Area is breathtaking in its own right. The southern frontier of the Annapurna Massif keeps you company for most of the journey, as winding roads, carved into the sides of hills, slowly lead you towards Nayapul, one of the traditional entry points into the ACA.

Nayapul was once the classic starting point for the trek to Annapurna Base Camp. Over the years, though, the starting point has gradually shifted. Today, there are multiple ways to begin; some start from Ulleri, others from Ghorepani or Ghandruk. More recently, Jhinu Danda has become a popular choice, where we chose to begin.

As you begin ascending from Nayapul, within just a few minutes, you catch your first clear glimpse of Machapuchare. With the iconic twin peaks that resemble a fishtail, which is its namesake, cutting cleanly into the sky, it feels almost unreal at first, too perfectly shaped, too still.

As we progressed, the roads told their own story. Some stretches were properly constructed, smooth and reassuring, while others felt half-baked, as I would say, rough patches of earth and gravel, uneven and relentlessly bumpy. At times, the road narrowed so much that only a single vehicle could pass, with steep drops waiting just beside you. Eventually, we reached the jeep park at Jhinu Danda.

We stopped at a small restaurant just a few steps from the jeep park, laid out our gear, and began the final preparations. Straps were tightened, bags were adjusted, essentials checked and rechecked. And then, we set out. Standing before the long suspension bridge, stretching nearly 290 meters across the valley, we paused to embrace and absorb the moment. The river roared far below, distant yet constant, while the mountains stood in stillness around us. We set out on this monumental journey to the top.

We stepped onto the suspension bridge and moved forward. The bridge swayed as fellow trekkers and porters passed by, and a cool mountain wind rushed through the valley between the hills and brushed past us, revitalising our minds, as if it is calling us towards the journey ahead.

On the other side, the stairs began. Endless, relentless, rising without pause. Treks in this region are known for these stone steps, carved into the mountainside, stretching far beyond what the eye can measure. They do not ease you in; they demand every bit of your respect and your effort.

We set our target for the day as Lower Sinuwa, with Chhomrong as our backup, in case the mountains had other plans, whether through fatigue or weather. Our approach was simple, no rush, slow and steady, and to listen to our body at all times. From Jhinu Danda to Chhomrong, the stairs rose without interruption. Thousands of steps, each one demanding each one taking its share of energy, just to reach the first village.

We kept pushing forward, step by step, slowly carving our way upward towards Chhomrong. It felt like a constant uphill battle, one that tested not just the body, but the mind as well.

The weather, for now, was on our side. Bright and sunny. But in the distance, we could see rain clouds beginning to gather, quiet and ominous. A reminder that in the mountains, nothing stays the same for long, and the weather can shift in moments.

We took breaks along the way whenever we could find a patch of shade, small pauses to catch our breath and steady the rhythm. And as we kept moving forward, the first droplets of rain began to fall. Soft at first, almost hesitant, but enough to signal what was coming. It was time to take out our ponchos and continue.

We pushed on through the drizzle, step by step, as the body slowly began to protest. Fatigue crept in quietly, settling into the legs, into the shoulders. Soon, we reached a small lodge and decided to rest for a while. Not long after, the rain intensified, as if the clouds had finally decided to let loose everything at once.

Rain in the mountains feels different. Sharper. Colder. The water numbs your hands with just a touch, and then, almost unexpectedly, snow begins to mix in, small flakes drifting down with the rain, as if the sky cannot decide what to be.

We chose to stay put, waiting for the storm to ease, watching and listening as it poured around us. It took around thirty minutes for the rain to lose some of its force, though even then, it never truly stopped. We were close. Just fifteen or twenty minutes away from Chhomrong. Close enough to feel it, but far enough for the final stretch to still demand everything we had left.

As the rain softened, we made the push. One last effort, tired legs carrying us forward, until finally, we reached Chhomrong…..
