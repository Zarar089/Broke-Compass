Pursuit of the Foothills of Annapurna · Part IV

Where the Snow Was Waiting

Dovan · Himalaya · Deurali

April 2026 7 min read

We reached Dovan at around 4:30 in the afternoon, with the rain still falling steadily. My coworkers were already there ahead of me, and they had taken care of booking the rooms.

This would be a good time to introduce them(since I hadn’t done it yet mistakenly), as the story ahead would be much easier to tell with their names mentioned. As I mentioned earlier I was accompanied on this trek by my coworkers, namely Nafis bhai and Soummo.

Nafis Bhai, being the lean and fit guy in the group, moved with remarkable ease along the trail, often pulling ahead with a steady and efficient pace. Soummo, too, could easily outpace me, but the trail would mesmerize him enough to halt him at times, drawn to the views, capturing moments that the rest of us simply walked past, which at times caused him to fall behind.

My own pace was different. I moved steadily, but with a habit of taking short breaks every 10 to 15 minutes. It slowed me down, yet in a way, it created a rhythm of its own, one that often allowed Soummo to catch up and move ahead again.

Over time, a pattern naturally formed. I would often start strong, pushing ahead at the beginning of the day or just after breaks, but as the hours wore on, I would gradually fall behind, ending the day trailing the pack. In between, our positions would constantly shift, shaped by pace, pauses, and the quiet demands of the mountains.

Once we reached Dovan, we decided to call it a day. We settled into our rooms, unpacked, and allowed ourselves some rest after a long and demanding stretch on the trail. By then, we were already halfway in, steadily drawing closer to the grandeur of Annapurna.

The rain lingered for hours, and as the evening deepened around 5:00 pm, it only grew heavier. The temperature dropped with it, wrapping the surroundings in a damp, biting cold that made a warm cup of coffee feel almost necessary.

The lodge had a small coffee shop of its own, run by one of the owners. As my coffee was being prepared, the man behind the counter, Prashant Dai, struck up a conversation. What began as a simple exchange soon turned into a long and engaging discussion about life, culture, and the mountains we were walking through. He shared stories about the lodge, built by his father nearly forty years ago, and spoke about the rhythms of life in these remote regions. In return, I shared a few stories of my own, the kind that naturally surface when strangers meet on a trail far from home.

Encounters like these became a quiet highlight of the journey. Along the way, we met several such people, each leaving behind a small but lasting impression. There was also a group of fellow countrymen trekking toward ABC, and our paths kept crossing every now and then, familiar faces appearing in unexpected places, adding a sense of shared journey to the solitude of the mountains.

One of the most memorable interactions we had along the trail came through a shared love for football. It was fascinating how, even in such remote corners of the mountains, the game managed to connect complete strangers. Conversations would spark instantly, sometimes leading to laughter, quick debates, and more often than not, a few selfies to remember the moment.

Simple exchanges became a recurring joy. A passing hello, a quick “hi,” or the familiar “where are you from” turned into brief but meaningful connections. Occasionally, these would grow into longer conversations with fellow trekkers moving in the opposite direction, each interaction adding a small but vibrant layer to the journey.

After the conversation, I headed back to our room. We had dinner soon after and turned in early, knowing the next day’s route would be demanding.

Morning came quickly. We woke up, finished our breakfast, and set out once again along the trail. The path led us deeper into the forest, with oak and rhododendron trees lining the way. The rhododendrons had just begun to bloom, their soft bursts of red and pink adding a quiet contrast to the earthy greens and browns around us.

The trail wound steadily ahead, weaving through streams and stretches of stone steps. Below us, the river rushed relentlessly through the valley, its sound echoing upward as a constant companion. Gradually, the dense forest began to thin, giving way to more open stretches that revealed glimpses of the vast landscape. Across the valley, the hills rose higher and sharper, their distant peaks lightly dusted with snow, like a delicate layer of icing resting on top.

Halfway along the trail to Himalaya, the presence of prayer flags began to grow. What started as a few scattered strands soon turned into rows upon rows of color, gently guiding the way toward the Pojenghem Baraha Temple, hidden deep within the forest.

The temple stood quietly, built from mountain stone in a traditional Nepali pagoda style. Red grills framed it on all four sides, while small bells hung around, swaying lightly with the wind. There was a stillness to the place, yet it felt alive in its own subtle way.

Just beside it, across the rushing Gandaki River, a waterfall revealed itself. It was not a single stream, but many, cascading down from the top of the hill, clinging to the rock face before falling into the river below. It spread out across the slope in a way that almost resembled an octopus, its many arms reaching downward in delicate, flowing lines.

Surrounded by blooming rhododendrons, the temple, the waterfall, and the forest came together in a moment that felt almost unreal. The flutter of prayer flags, the gentle chime of bells, and the deep, constant rush of the river created a harmony that was both calming and overwhelming.

Most trekkers would pause here, if only for a few moments. Some would offer prayers, others would simply stay still, taking it all in. We did the same. We rested, took a few photographs, let the moment settle within us, and then quietly continued on our way.

Right after the temple, the stairs made its dramatic return. It seemed endless, after we climbed a few flights and as it seemed like there weren’t anymore, more flights of stairs came into sight. The ascent continued, relentless, demanding, every step a battle against gravity. Another new challenge was slowly seeping in, altitude. We were gradually nearing the 3000 metre mark. The next stop itself sat at 2900 meters. We had already taken necessary precautions the day before. As we followed the daunting stairs we reached another clearing from where we could already see our next station, Himalaya nearby.

Progressing steadily, we eventually reached Himalaya, a small stop with only a handful of lodges, yet easily one of the most exquisite along the trail. By this point, the towering peaks seemed to retreat behind nearer ridges, revealing themselves only in fragments as we moved closer into their domain.

As we arrived, something in the distance caught my eye. It was not a mountain or a waterfall, but a flag, faint yet familiar. Drawn by curiosity, I moved closer, and there was no mistaking it. It was the Bangladeshi flag, standing along with flags from other countries.

Deep within the Himalayas, at an altitude of nearly 2900 meters, it stood tall, fluttering freely against the strong mountain winds. In that quiet, unexpected moment, far from home, a momentary sense of patriotism rose within me, simple and unspoken, yet impossible to ignore.

After a brief stop for a warm drink, we set off again with Deurali as our next destination. Before long, the trail reminded us of a familiar adversary, the endless stone stairs. By now, they had begun to inspire a quiet resentment, each step demanding more than the last. Yet, with steady resolve, we climbed on, pushing against gravity in a rhythm that had become second nature.

The ascent led us to a curious resting point. A small shrine stood there, a Buddha idol placed upon broad, natural stone steps. Above it loomed a massive rock, textured like the scales of a fish, arching outward to form a shelter. It was said to serve as a resting place for porters, its overhang offering protection from rain, snow, and the harsh mountain sun. From this vantage, Deurali appeared close, almost within reach.

After a short pause, we continued along the trail, crossing streams fed by cascading waterfalls that clung to the mountainside. The path rose once more in a final stretch of stairs, and soon we arrived at Deurali, standing at 3200 meters above sea level.

Here, for the first time in my life, I saw snow resting quietly on the ground. It had fallen the night before, leaving behind soft patches gathered along the edges of the trail. Having only ever seen snow through screens, the moment felt quietly surreal. I could not resist the temptation. Within seconds, I was shaping snowballs and tossing them toward my companions, laughter briefly replacing the fatigue of the climb.

After a few minutes of this childish indulgence, we settled down for lunch. The afternoon was already beginning to lean toward evening, and a decision awaited us. Should we stay the night here, or push onward to Machapuchare Base Camp? The information we had suggested it was a two-hour trek from this point. By our estimates, we believed we could make it before dark.

What we did not yet realise was that the most demanding stretch of the entire journey still lay ahead.
